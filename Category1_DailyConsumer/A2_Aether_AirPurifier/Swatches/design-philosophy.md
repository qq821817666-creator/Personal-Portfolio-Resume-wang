# Material Lexicon

**A design philosophy for the systematic veneration of color, material, and finish.**

---

Color and material are not decoration — they are a precise language. When we catalogue them with the rigor of scientific observation, we reveal their true power: each swatch a phoneme, each texture a syntax, each finish a dialect. This philosophy treats the CMF palette not as a mood board but as a taxonomic field guide — an artifact that proves the ephemeral qualities of touch, light, and surface can be studied, mapped, and understood through careful, reverent attention.

**Space operates as a domestic sanctuary.** The canvas breathes with the quiet confidence of a curated interior. Generous margins create visual quietude around each specimen, allowing individual material swatches to resonate without competition — much like a well-designed room where every object earns its place. The layout follows a grid of almost-mathematical precision — not rigid, but inevitable — as if each element arrived at its position through natural law rather than compositional choice. Negative space is not empty; it is the atmosphere in which natural materials truthfully reveal themselves against controlled neutrals.

**Color is the primary information system — drawn from nature, not the color wheel.** Each swatch exists as both data point and domestic object. Body colors claim the largest circles — dominant, anchoring, the first read. Natural wood tones and stone grays orbit as companions, their warm undertones shifting perceptibly against the body hue. Textured rectangles for fabric felt and natural bases complete each variant's material story. Together, the five material zones per variant form a complete sentence in the language of home: wood, textile, stone, ceramic, light. The progression from warm to cool, from hard to soft, from natural to engineered is encoded chromatically and spatially.

**The material hierarchy reflects domestic reality.** Wood-fiber composite (body) is the largest surface — like flooring or wall treatment. Acoustic felt (grille) is the soft textile element — like upholstery or curtains. Ceramic (control ring) is the crafted touchpoint — like pottery or tableware. Bamboo/walnut/cork (base) grounds the object — like furniture legs. Frosted PMMA (LED) is the ambient light source — like a window or lamp. Every material has a domestic reference. Nothing reads as "appliance."

**Typography serves as scientific annotation, never narration.** Labels are whispered in precise sans-serif — Pantone codes, CIELAB coordinates, gloss unit readings, surface roughness values. Text appears only where measurement meets material, never explaining what the eye already knows. Chinese and English coexist in bilingual harmony, each serving the same documentary function with equal visual weight. Type size hierarchies follow a strict logic: variant names command the most presence, material zones follow, technical specifications recede to the threshold of legibility — present for those who seek them, invisible to those who simply feel.

**The artifact must appear meticulously crafted**, the product of countless refinements by someone at the absolute top of their field. Every swatch is perfectly circular, every rectangle precisely proportioned, every alignment the result of obsessive calibration. Color values are reproduced with spectrophotometric fidelity. The separation between elements — measured in millimeters, adjusted by instinct — creates a rhythm that rewards sustained viewing. This is not a document; it is a proof specimen. Evidence that the invisible qualities of surface and light deserve the same reverence we reserve for observable phenomena — and that a machine for cleaning air can be as thoughtfully materialized as a piece of furniture.
